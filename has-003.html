HAS-003

Prompted Self-Disclosure and OSINT Surface Expansion

Framework

Human Attack Surface (HAS)

Context (abstracted)

Environment: casual learning / mentorship interaction
Medium: chat + collaborative build (“all about me” style page)
Relationship state: learner seeking guidance from a more experienced peer
Data type: biographical trivia and “security question–adjacent” details

Hypothesis

Friendly onboarding prompts (especially in learning contexts) normalize the disclosure of personal details. When aggregated, these details expand OSINT surface area and can unintentionally resemble password-recovery signals or future authentication hints.

Observations

A project framed as harmless and fun (“all about me”) used prompts that invited specific personal facts: names, preferences, favorites, and identity-adjacent details. The tone was casual and instructional, and the activity felt like normal beginner practice rather than data collection.

Because the prompts were presented as standard onboarding content, each answer felt low-risk in isolation. The risk became visible only when considering how easily the information could be aggregated, searched, or re-used as verification “facts” in other contexts.

Trigger Conditions

Authority gradient: guidance coming from someone positioned as a teacher/mentor increased compliance.
Play framing: “fun” prompts reduced perceived threat and lowered scrutiny.
Chunking effect: many small disclosures felt harmless, but combined into a meaningful profile.
Future visibility: publishing or sharing artifacts makes private trivia searchable and persistent.

Human Factors Identified

Compliance under instruction • harmlessness bias • aggregation blindness • authority effect • persistence neglect

Systemic Failure Mode

The failure is not technical—it's a design and culture gap. Beginner projects often treat personal trivia as “safe demo data,” even though many systems and communities still use personal facts as identity anchors. The learning objective (practice forms/inputs/UI) unintentionally trains disclosure habits that generalize.

Defensive Implications

Replace real personal facts with safe placeholders in tutorials (“favorite color” becomes “favorite IDE theme”, names become “Sample User A”). Treat “all about me” prompts as production-grade data collection: minimize, abstract, and avoid security-question-adjacent facts entirely.

Communities and instructors should explicitly teach the concept of aggregation risk: individual facts feel harmless, but bundles become identifiers. When publishing portfolios or demo apps, use fictional profiles.

Ethical Notes

No identifying details are included. No tactics are provided to target individuals. This experiment documents first-person observations and is published solely for defensive awareness and education.
